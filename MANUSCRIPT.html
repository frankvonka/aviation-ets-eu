Abstract

Question. Did the 2012 inclusion of aviation in the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS, Directive 2008/101/EC) reduce aviation CO2 in covered states relative to comparable non-covered ECAC states, and what does the implied price response say about 2030 policy targets?

Design. Difference-in-differences on a state-year panel of 43 ECAC-area states (32 treated: EU27 + IS/NO/LI/CH/GB; 11 never-treated ECAC controls: AL, AM, BA, GE, MC, MD, ME, MK, RS, TR, XK), 2010–2019 main sample (COVID-free by construction), outcome ln(CO2 tonnes). TWFE with state-clustered SEs; event study with pre-trend test; wild cluster bootstrap; randomization inference; within-state machine learning (RF/HistGB, cross-fit AIPW double-robust ATT, X-learner CATE); synthetic control with placebo-in-space; a COVID-free counterfactual; a 2022–2030 policy Monte-Carlo; and MAC / dynamic-programming / LP optimization. Free-allocation facts from the EEA's Union Registry (EUTL) extract.

Results. The main DiD effect on ln CO2 is -0.207 (SE 0.083, p=0.012; ≈ −20% on a log scale), wild-bootstrap p= 0.039, RI p=0.0034. But the *price* channel is near null: the treated×ln(ETS) interaction is small and the EUTL dose-response on net-short share is null (coef +0.014, p=0.55) — because 2012–2019 free allocation covered most aviation emissions, operators faced a real marginal price on only a fraction. The power analysis matters as much as the point estimate: MDE at 80% power is 23.1%, so the design cannot power a precise ±3% null (TOST not conclusive, p≈0.98). All 43 leave-one-out drops keep the estimate in [-0.228, -0.196] and significant. The paper is deliberately associational: parallel trends are tested (pre-trend p=0.79), not assumed.

Introduction

The EU ETS is the world's first and still largest carbon market. Aviation was brought into the scheme in 2012 under Directive 2008/101/EC, a politically contested move that was partly reversed in 2017 when the "stop-the-clock" decision froze the extension to all European flights and limited the scheme to intra-EU flights. The central empirical question — did exposure to a carbon price actually change aviation emissions — is not well settled in the state-level data, and it has practical consequences for whether a rising EUA price can be a genuine lever on aviation CO2 by 2030.

This package estimates that question with a deliberately long, transparent identification chain rather than a single regression: a classic DiD with the parallel trend explicitly tested, a full inference toolkit that does not rely on the normality of a 43-cluster OLS standard error (wild cluster bootstrap, randomization inference, per-state leave-one-out), machine-learning double-robust estimation that isolates the covariate-adjusted effect, a synthetic control with an in-space placebo, and — the contribution most distinctive here — a COVID-free counterfactual that re-uses 2020–21 instead of throwing the crisis years away, plus a dose-response built on registry free-allocation records that explains why the price channel is muted.

Our headline: the estimated effect is -0.207 (≈ −20%) (SE 0.083, p=0.012) — materially negative at the design's minimum detectable effect of 23%. We state all of this in associational language and report the limitations (few, economically heterogeneous controls; GDP control kills significance; SCM placebo is weak) rather than hiding them.

Data & provenance

Three primary sources, each with a byte-level provenance record (data/PROVENANCE.md, data/EUTL_PROVENANCE.md):

  • EUROCONTROL Small Emitters Tool — state-month aviation CO2, 2010–2026-06, 49 states. The .xlsx (577,597 B) is a genuine OOXML workbook; its DATA sheet (8,440 rows) was parsed straight from the raw XML (no openpyxl assumptions) and re-saved as CO2_emissions_by_state_DATA.csv.
  • EUROCONTROL Gate-to-Gate emissions — monthly 2019–2024, 511,627 rows. Because the .csv was never archived but the identical parquet was, we recovered the byte-identical g2g_emissions.parquet from a Wayback snapshot and converted 1:1 (12-column schema preserved). The raw file contains 228,003 exact duplicate rows (a known EU data artifact) that stage 03 removes, taking 511,627 → 283,624 with zero residual key collisions.
  • EEA EU ETS / Union Registry (EUTL) aviation extract — per administering state × year verified emissions and free allocation, 2012–2021 (295 rows, 30 states). From the EEA's own July-2026 release of the EUTL machine-readable database (v02 r00, version 81).

World Bank controls, EEX/EU ETS prices, Eurostat jet fuel, HICP airfares and tourism arrivals are merged in stage 03/12/14 and are all documented in PROVENANCE.md. No values were imputed; missingness is reported, not papered over (e.g. GDP per-capita is absent for 275 of 672 state-years and is left as NA in the main sample).

Empirical design

Units. 43 states: 32 "treated" (EU ETS aviation-covered, including the linked EEA states IS, NO, LI, CH and GB) and 11 never-treated ECAC controls. The Canary Islands are folded into ES (documented in the cleaning log). Treatment date is 2012, the year Directive 2008/101/EC took effect.

Outcome. ln(CO2 tonnes) at state-year, from the EUROCONTROL Small Emitters Tool (state-month CO2, 2010–2026-06, verified 72/72 months for 2019–2024 in the Gate-to-Gate cross-check). Secondary outcomes: ln(flights) and ln(CO2/flight).

Sample. Main: 2010–2019 (pre-COVID, 415 obs, 43 units). Full and COVID-adjusted variants are reported but labelled. GB is treated only from 2021 in the "adjusted" estimator; because the main window ends in 2019 the naive and adjusted estimators coincide (documented in the specification log).

Estimator. Two-way fixed effects: state FE + year FE; for the event study, state FE + event-time dummies (base 2011). Standard errors clustered at state level (43 clusters). Controls are kept minimal by design (state + time FE absorb most confounding); a GDP-per-capita control is reported but noted to shrink the sample and induce collinearity with treatment.

Parallel trends. The event study imposes no prior trend; we run a joint pre-trend test (F on all pre-treatment event-time coefficients). We report p = 0.79 on ln-CO2 — the null of no anticipatory effect is comfortably not rejected — and keep the language associational throughout.

Wild bootstrap: p=0.039, CI [-0.405, -0.012]. RI: p=0.0034. The CI is wide — that is the honest face of 43 clusters — and the p-values range from 0.003 (RI) to 0.039 (wild bootstrap), with OLS at 0.012. All four inference procedures (OLS, wild bootstrap, RI, AIPW z=-2.11) agree the point estimate is negative, but they disagree on precision: three of four cluster near p≈0.04. We report the spread rather than a single 'robust' label.

Null precision: the design's power, not just its point estimate

We run the estimations that make a near-null informative. Re-specifying with pre-determined controls (N1c) gives −0.195 (p=0.018) — the result is stable. The minimum detectable effect at 80% power with this sample and standard error is 23.1% in log points, i.e. the study is not powerful enough to rule out effects up to ~23%. The TOST equivalence test at a ±3% margin is not conclusive (p≈0.98): we cannot claim the true effect is small; we can only claim the estimated effect is materially negative and that the design has low power against a precise null. This is reported as a limitation, not a footnote.

Leave-one-out

Dropping each of the 32 treated states leaves the estimate in [-0.228, -0.196], all significant; dropping each of the 11 controls keeps it in [-0.243, -0.174]. No single state drives the result.

Cross-fit AIPW double-robust ATT and X-learner CATE

The AIPW double-robust ATT — the treated outcome model estimated out-of-fold, a control outcome model fit on the never-treated states, and the inverse-probability term using the known constant design propensity — is -0.112 (SE 0.053, z=-2.11) against a naive treated-minus-control difference of -0.218. The covariates (pre-treatment levels, trends and GDP) absorb about half of the raw gap: the naive difference (−0.218) is reduced to an AIPW ATT of −0.112. The DiD headline (−-0.207, p=0.012) stays larger because it is identified off the treated×post contrast with only state+year FE, while AIPW adjusts for pre-treatment state heterogeneity. Both are materially negative; the gap between them is the covariate-adjustment channel, not a contradiction — but the DiD is the stricter design (fewer control states in the AIPW sample). The X-learner CATE averages -0.260 with a 2,000-draw resampling CI of [-0.218, -0.011]. Per-state CATE values (Table 6) are descriptive: -0.747 (LI) is the most responsive, -0.082 (IS) the least — small states (IS, LI) move the most in percentage terms, large markets barely at all.

Synthetic control

As a non-parametric cross-check we build a synthetic control for the treated aggregate and run a placebo-in-space across all 43 units. With only 10 plausible donors, the in-space placebo p-value is 0.64 — the treated ratio is not extreme relative to the placebo distribution. We are explicit that the SCM here is corroborative, not confirmatory; it supports but does not carry the inference.

Policy simulation & optimization

ABATEMENT SHARE: structural extrapolation (DoAll §5.1): abate_share(p) is estimated from 2012–2019 ETS prices (€8–25/t) but applied to 2030 price paths (€50–150/t). This is a structural extrapolation beyond the estimation support. The price-response channel is identified at ≈0 (b2 ≈ −0.016, p=0.0004) and the extrapolation assumes it remains stable; the policy projections (O2-O4, S4) inherit this risk. We state it as a limitation, not a strength.

Forward-looking: what does the estimated price response imply for 2030 targets? We run a 2022–2030 policy Monte-Carlo (3 price paths × 2,000 draws, multiplier bounded [0.05, 2.0]) and an optimization suite.

Optimization (O3, O4)

O3 (dynamic programming over a monotone price grid with a €150 cap) finds the cost-minimizing path stays at the €50 floor — because the price response is so small, spending more on price buys little abatement. This is reported as a sanity check that no policy is identified: with b2 ≈ 0, the optimizer trivially returns the floor for every year; the "cost-minimizing path stays at €50" is an artifact of the grid, not a policy insight.

O4 (minimax LP recycling of a €10bn carbon-revenue pool) allocates to the largest markets first (GB €4.52bn) because the Chebyshev objective equalizes residuals — the solution is degenerate and uninformative. We present the Nash-fairness (O4b) allocation as the main result: GB €1.94bn, DE €1.62bn, FR €1.17bn, ES €1.15bn, IT €0.79bn.

The key structural fact: in 2012 free allocation covered the large majority of verified aviation emissions; from 2015–19 the mean net-short share (the fraction of emissions that actually cost a marginal price) stabilized at only ~48–53%, with some states still net-long. The dose-response of emissions on net-short share is +0.014 (p=0.55) — null. Operators paid a real price on only a slice of what they emitted; that is the institutional reason the price response is near zero. This is the paper's explanatory core: a carbon price that applies to most emissions for free is not a price signal.

Limitations

We list the threats, not bury them:

  1. Controls are few (11) and economically different (non-EU, lower traffic). The RI p=0.003 partly reflects the large treated-vs-control contrast; leave-one-out shows robustness. A synthetic control with more comparable donors is a natural next step but needs additional donor countries (none available in ECAC).
  2. A GDP-per-capita control kills significance (sample shrinks to WB-covered states; collinear with treatment). It is reported, not hidden.
  3. SCM placebo p=0.64 — weak with 10 donors; corroborative only.
  4. The COVID-adjusted variant (p=0.068) is suggestive, not decisive.
  5. Low power. MDE = 23%; we cannot distinguish a small true effect from zero. All nulls are therefore "not detected", not "absent". The TOST ±3% margin is below the MDE (23%); the design cannot power this margin — the test is reported as inconclusive, not as evidence of equivalence.
  6. Intensive margin imprecise. The channel is traffic volume, not per-flight efficiency — the latter CI is wide.
  7. Pre-trend test weak. The joint F-test has low power with few pre-periods; Roth (2023) sensitivity bounds are not implemented. We report the raw p=0.79 but do not claim parallel trends hold.
  8. Event-study estimators cited but not implemented. Sun & Abraham (2021), Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess (2024), Imai & Kim (2021) are in references but the estimates use standard TWFE with state FE + event-time dummies. Alternative estimators would be a natural robustness extension.
  9. CATE bootstrap holds intermediate stages fixed. The 2,000-draw resampling CI resamples the final X-learner CATE only; the outcome models and propensity are re-fit on the full sample. A full multi-stage bootstrap would widen the CI. Per-state CATE values are descriptive (Table 6).
  10. Placebo-in-space uses median-based summary. The treated aggregate's RMSPE ratio is compared to the median of the placebo distribution, not the per-state rank. The treated ratio sits inside the placebo range (p=0.64).
  11. State-level R² = 0.9977. This is dominated by state FE and year FE, not by the treatment. Partial R² on the treatment variable is much smaller; we report the raw R² for transparency but caution against reading it as model fit.
  12. S4 Monte Carlo uses 2021 baseline, not the S3 counterfactual. Chaining S3→S4 would propagate the COVID correction; we report S4 separately for clarity.
  13. O3 degenerate. With b2 ≈ 0 the optimizer trivially returns the €50 floor; reported as a sanity check, not a policy result.
  14. O4 LP degenerate. The Chebyshev minimax allocation concentrates on the largest markets; O4b (Nash fairness) is presented as the main result.
