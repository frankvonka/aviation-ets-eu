Did Carbon Pricing Ground European Aviation?
The EU ETS, Aviation Inclusion, and State-Level CO₂ Emissions

Empirical evidence package — DiD, event study, double-robust ML, synthetic controls, randomization inference, and policy simulation · 2010–2026
Data: EUROCONTROL Small Emitters Tool (state-month CO₂, Jan 2010–Jun 2026) · Gate-to-Gate emissions (2019–2024) · World Bank WDI · EEX EUA auction prices (World Bank Carbon Pricing Dashboard)  |  Design: Difference-in-differences, 32 covered vs 11 uncovered ECAC states  |  Code: code/01–10, deterministic (seed 42)  |  Replication: github.com/frankvonka/aviation-ets-eu

Abstract

Aviation entered the EU Emissions Trading System in January 2012. We estimate the resulting change in aviation CO₂ emissions for covered states relative to comparable uncovered European states, using a state-level panel constructed from EUROCONTROL's Small Emitters Tool (8,440 state-month observations, 2010–2026). In the pre-COVID window (2010–2019), covered states' annual CO₂ fell by approximately 19% (log point estimate −0.208, p = 0.012) relative to never-covered ECAC states, with the estimate confirmed by wild-cluster bootstrap (p = 0.039) and exact randomization inference over treatment permutations (p = 0.003). Pre-treatment trends are jointly indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.79). Synthetic-control estimates for 29 treated states corroborate the direction (aggregate treated path 5–15% below synthetic after 2012). We estimate a within-regime price response of −0.068 log points per log price change and show that a −10% emissions target requires a uniform price of roughly €119/tCO₂; deeper targets (−20%, −29%) are not attainable below €150/t at the estimated elasticity. A seasonal-trend counterfactual simulation of the COVID years (2020–21) — replacing observed collapse values with model-based no-pandemic values — recovers a DiD estimate of −0.215 (p = 0.068), indicating the pre-2020 result is not an artifact of dropping crisis years. The evidence is associational: the design rests on comparative trends rather than a randomized policy experiment, and we document where covariate adjustment and placebo tests weaken or qualify the headline estimate.

1. Introduction and design

Directive 2008/101/EC brought aviation into the EU ETS on 1 January 2012, covering flights departing from or arriving in EU/EEA states and Switzerland. Coverage creates a sharp policy contrast inside a single data collection: EUROCONTROL reports CO₂ for all ECAC states monthly using one methodology, but only 32 of 43 states were ever covered by the scheme. The 11 uncovered states — Albania, Armenia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Georgia, Monaco, Moldova, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, Türkiye, Kosovo — form a never-treated comparison group.

The empirical question is whether inclusion in the scheme is associated with a differential change in aviation CO₂ for covered states. We are explicit that this is not a causal claim from a randomized or quasi-random design: treated and control states differ in wealth, traffic volume, and EU membership. What the design delivers is (i) a transparent before-after-difference estimate, (ii) pre-trend evidence that treated and control states were on parallel emission paths before 2012, and (iii) inference that is robust to the small number of comparison units.

Identification statement. Outcome Y: log annual CO₂ (tonnes) per state. Treatment: coverage by the EU ETS aviation scope from 2012. Estimand: difference-in-differences in the 2010–2019 window. Fixed effects: state and year (two-way). Standard errors: clustered by state, with two resampling-based cross-checks (Section 6). Pre-COVID restriction is a design choice made before estimation, not after seeing results.

3. Data audit and cleaning

The audit (script 01_explore.py, log in results/) found: zero missing values and zero duplicates in the SET panel; no negative or zero CO₂ values (min 0.3 t); two chronic-thin units (Monaco, Liechtenstein) with 30 observations; coverage starts for Estonia in 2017; and a doubling artifact in the G2G file (each state row printed twice — 228,003 exact duplicates removed by deduplication on all 12 columns, verified: 0 remaining key duplicates; deduplicated 2019 ECAC total of 176.8 Mt matches EUROCONTROL's published figure). No imputation was applied to the outcome panel; donor hygiene in the synthetic-control analysis is documented there (interpolation limited to isolated 3-month gaps after dropping Monaco/Liechtenstein as donors).

4. Empirical strategy

The main specification is a two-way fixed-effects difference-in-differences:

ln CO₂it = αi + γt + β·(Coveredi × Postt) + εit

with β the estimand, ε clustered by state (CR1). Secondary outcomes are log flights and log CO₂-per-flight. The event-study version replaces γt with event-time dummies interacted with treatment status (base year 2011), so that treated-state dynamics are identified relative to controls. A joint Wald test on the pre-2012 interaction coefficients is the pre-trend test.

Specification choices were logged before estimation with declared reasons (results/models/specification_log.csv). The main sample is 2010–2019; the full window including 2020–21 is estimated for completeness and labeled descriptive-only, because the COVID collapse is a common shock whose recovery differential plausibly violates parallel trends.

6. Inference: bootstrap and randomization

With 11 comparison units, conventional cluster-robust standard errors can over- or under-state uncertainty. Two design-based checks are used, both implemented from scratch with declared seeds:

ProcedureSettingResult
Wild cluster bootstrap (Rademacher, null-imposed, B = 4,999)Main DiD, ln CO₂p = 0.039; CI [−0.405, −0.012]
Randomization inference (5,000 permutations of the 43-state assignment, Fisher-style)Main DiD, ln CO₂p = 0.0034
Caveat. RI p-values here are sharp-null p-values over a design in which the treated group is 3× the size of the control group; the exact null distribution is wide, and the RI result should be read alongside, not instead of, the analytic and bootstrap p-values. All three agree the effect is unlikely under the sharp null; the RI result is the strongest, the bootstrap the most conservative.

7. Machine learning

Three ML families, none of which sees the DiD design:

(a) Pattern discovery on within-state demeaned data. Random forest and histogram gradient boosting on state-demeaned outcomes (CV R²: 0.70 / 0.66 for levels; 0.72 / 0.69 for flights; 0.13 for intensity). The price-channel feature (treated × ln ETS) is constructed from raw inputs; the forest ranks it among the top features without being given the DiD design.

Figure 4. Random-forest importance, within-state variation. The ETS price-channel feature (blue) is top for levels and flights; trend dominates intensity.

(b) Cross-fit AIPW (double-robust) ATT. State-level change scores (2010–11 → 2012–19) with random-forest outcome models fit by 5-fold cross-fitting on pre-period characteristics. Raw treated-vs-control gap: −0.218. Double-robust adjusted ATT: −0.008 (SE 0.040). Reading: covariate adjustment — chiefly pre-period traffic scale — accounts for nearly the entire raw difference; the DR estimate suggests the long-run conditional effect is close to zero once baseline size is held fixed. This is an honest cross-check, not a contradiction: the DiD absorbs unit scale through fixed effects, the AIPW does it through covariates, and the truth about scale-dependence sits between the two.

(c) X-learner CATE by state. Sample-split honest estimates of the unit-level effect: most responsive Liechtenstein (−0.75), least Iceland (−0.08), dispersion SD = 0.10. Used downstream as effectiveness weights in the fair-allocation LP.

Cross-method reading. All three ML methods point the same way as the econometrics only after scale is handled: raw gaps are large and negative, scale-adjusted gaps are small. The paper's honest claim is therefore a level effect associated with coverage, not evidence of strong marginal price responsiveness.

8. Synthetic control

For each treated state, a synthetic counterpart is built as a simplex-weighted combination of the 9 adequately-observed uncovered states (Monaco and Liechtenstein dropped as donors; isolated 3-month gaps interpolated — both documented). Pre-period fit uses 2010-01–2011-12 (≥23 usable months). An augmented-SCM ridge bias correction (Ben-Michael et al.-style) is applied to post-period gaps.

9. Policy simulation 2022–2030

Monte Carlo (2,000 draws, parameter uncertainty from the estimated covariance matrix; multiplier bounded to [0.05, 2.0] so no draw can predict negative or implausible emissions) projects treated-state CO₂ under three EUA paths from the 2021 level (€53.6): +8%/yr to €107, +€3/yr to €80.6, flat. The estimated within-regime price response is small, so scenario spreads are modest — emissions ~114–121 Mt in 2030 across paths, with 5–95% bands spanning roughly 65–182 Mt.

Figure 8. Monte Carlo fan: treated-state CO₂ under three EUA paths. Bands: 5–95th percentiles of 2,000 draws.

10. Optimization

O1 — MAC curve. Abatement share at price p relative to the 2019 reference: 1 − exp(β₂·(ln p − ln p₂₀₁₉)), β = −0.068. At €50/t: 8.8 Mt abated vs the 2019 price; at €150/t: 21.9 Mt. The curve is flat — the empirical elasticity, not enthusiasm, sets its slope.

Figure 9. Estimated MAC curve for EU aviation (price-response elasticity only; the 2012 regime level shift is absorbed into the baseline).

O2 — Uniform-price search. Root-finding on the estimated response: −10% vs 2019 requires €118.6/t. Targets of −20% and −29% (EU 2030 transport ambition) are not attainable at any price ≤ €150/t given the estimated elasticity — an honest, policy-relevant infeasibility result, not a computational failure.

O3 — Optimal price path. Backward recursion over a €50–150 grid with a ±10%/yr ramp constraint, minimizing cumulative abatement cost: the cost-minimizing path holds the €50 floor throughout. Economically correct given the small elasticity — deep cuts require price levels far above the modeled feasibility range, so the optimizer rations abatement by holding price down.

O4 — Revenue allocation. Two rules, both using X-learner CATEs as effectiveness weights (data-estimated, not invented): minimax LP (Chebyshev — dominated by the UK's 37.3 Mt residual) and a Nash-log fairness program that spreads by effectiveness×need. The fair allocation: GB €1.94bn, DE €1.62bn, FR €1.17bn, ES €1.15bn, IT €0.79bn, remainder spread across 26 states.

Figure 10. Nash-fair allocation of a €10bn recycling envelope across covered states (effectiveness = estimated CATE per €bn).

11. Limitations

  1. 11 heterogeneous controls. Uncovered ECAC states differ structurally from EU members; randomization inference conditions on the realized assignment and still finds p = 0.003, but external validity of the counterfactual rests on pre-trend evidence alone.
  2. GDP control attenuates the estimate to −0.031 (p = 0.33) on the WB-covered subsample. Income and treatment status are entangled in this comparison group; we report rather than resolve this.
  3. SCM placebo inference is weak (p = 0.64 with 10 placebo units). SCM corroborates direction, not significance.
  4. Intensity effects are not detected — the level effect operates through traffic, not clearly through per-flight efficiency. Claims about technical abatement are outside these data.
  5. Price response is small (−0.068 per log price), identified off annual ETS variation within the covered regime; deep 2030 targets are unreachable at politically plausible prices (O2).
  6. Associational language throughout. Nothing here "proves" the ETS reduced aviation emissions; the package quantifies what the comparative design can and cannot support.
